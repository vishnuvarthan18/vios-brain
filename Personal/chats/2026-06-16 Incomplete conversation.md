## Lessons with solid scientific backing

**1. "Love failure is a superpower"** — Supported.
Carol Dweck's growth-mindset research (Stanford) shows that people who treat failure as information rather than verdict improve faster. In her studies, students praised for effort sought harder challenges and recovered from setbacks; those praised for ability avoided risk. The mechanism is real: error-related brain activity (the Pe signal, measured via EEG) predicts who corrects after a mistake.
*Example:* James Dyson built 5,126 failed prototypes before the working cyclone vacuum. Each failure narrowed the design space.
*Caveat:* failure only teaches if you extract the cause. Repeated failure without analysis is just loss.

**7 & 8. "Understand everyday" + "Go deep"** — Supported (this is deliberate practice).
Anders Ericsson's research established that expertise comes from focused, feedback-driven practice on specific weaknesses, not raw hours or talent. The popular "10,000 hours" rule is Gladwell's distortion of Ericsson's work — Ericsson himself rejected the fixed number. What matters is quality and specificity of practice.
*Example:* chess masters don't memorize more; they chunk board patterns from years of targeted study.

**9. "Patterns are everything"** — Supported.
This is pattern recognition / chunking, the cognitive basis of expertise (de Groot's chess studies, 1940s–60s). Experts see structure where novices see noise. Same finding in radiology, firefighting, trading.

**16. "There are good & bad seasons"** — Supported.
Hedonic adaptation (Brickman & Campbell) and the finding that affect mean-reverts. Lottery winners and accident victims both drift back toward baseline happiness within ~1–2 years. Emotional states are not permanent; this is well-replicated.

**21. "Storytelling is the shortcut"** — Supported.
Narrative is remembered far better than facts. Studies on the "narrative effect" show information embedded in story is recalled at multiples of isolated facts. Paul Zak's work suggests character-driven narrative raises oxytocin and shifts behavior (his effect sizes are debated, but the memory advantage is robust).

**23. "Think of your 8-year & 80-year-old self"** — Partially supported (temporal/future-self framing).
Hal Hershfield (UCLA) showed people who felt connected to their future self saved more money and made healthier choices. In one study, viewing age-progressed images of themselves increased retirement savings allocations. The "80-year-old self" half is evidence-based.

## Lessons that are reasonable but not scientific claims

**11. "If you're not failing, you're not winning"** — Partly true, overstated.
The kernel is real: optimal learning happens at ~85% success / 15% failure. Wilson et al. (2019) found machine and human learning is fastest at that error rate — which is also where lesson 24 ("8.5 rule") comes from; they're the same idea. But "not failing = not winning" as stated is false. Plenty of winning is consolidation, not risk.

**24. "Have an 8.5 rule"** — This *is* the 85% finding above. Aim for tasks you succeed at ~85% of the time. Genuinely useful and evidence-based.

**17. "You only do ~20 things every day"** — Reframed, this is real.
Decision/attention is finite. Roy Baumeister's ego-depletion work claimed willpower is a depletable resource — but note this failed major replication (2016 multi-lab study found near-zero effect). The *durable* version is the Pareto principle: a small share of actions produces most of your outcomes. Prioritize the few high-leverage ones.

**18. "Your new life costs your old one"** — Opportunity cost. Sound economics, not a lab result.

**19. "Stop playing victim"** — Maps loosely to locus of control (Rotter). Internal locus of control correlates with better health, achievement, and resilience across many studies. The reframe is fair.

**20. "Avoid the news"** — Partial support.
Negative-news consumption correlates with anxiety and distorted risk perception (e.g., people overestimate violent-crime risk). But "avoid" is too strong; the evidence supports limiting doom-scrolling, not blackout.

## Lessons that are opinion, not findings — and one that's harmful

**2. "Success does not change shit"** — Half-true. Hedonic adaptation (above) supports it: external success doesn't durably raise baseline happiness. But this is the same science as #16, restated.

**3. "Do not listen to parents"** — No scientific support; as a blanket rule it's wrong. The defensible version: don't let others' risk tolerance cap your decisions. Stated absolutely, it's bad advice.

**6. "College is mostly useless"** — Contradicted by data. The college wage premium is large and persistent (graduates earn substantially more lifetime; unemployment is consistently lower). It's *field- and debt-dependent*, not useless. This one is empirically false as written.

**14. "Friends forever is a myth"** — Loosely supported. Dunbar's research and longitudinal network studies show friendship circles turn over roughly every ~7 years and active relationships are capped (~150 total, ~5 close). The blunt phrasing overshoots, but turnover is real.

**15. "You cannot stop addiction"** — **Factually wrong and harmful.** Addiction is treatable. Recovery rates are substantial; most people with substance use disorders eventually achieve remission (national survey data). Medication-assisted treatment, CBT, and contingency management have strong evidence. This statement is dangerous as written.

**4, 5, 10, 12, 13, 22, 25** — Aphorisms, not testable claims. Some are decent heuristics (#12 ≈ "what gets measured gets managed," a management maxim with mixed evidence; #25 = memento mori, philosophy not science). None have a clean experimental result behind them.

**Bottom line:** the genuinely evidence-backed cluster is 1, 7–9, 16, 21, 23, and the 85% rule (11/24). Treat 6 and 15 as false. The rest are motivation, not data.
