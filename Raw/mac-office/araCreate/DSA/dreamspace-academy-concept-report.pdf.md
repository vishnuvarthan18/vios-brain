1.4 SUMMARY
This objective of this document to provide a comprehensive overview of DreamSpace
Academy's story, mission, vision, and empowerment activities. It records the organisation's
impact since its founding, outlines its past and future focus areas, and demonstrates how the
organisation is restructuring to achieve its goals.

Sri Lanka continues to grapple with systemic barriers to sustainable development, rooted in
its post-civil war socio-economic landscape and rigid institutional frameworks. Decades of
conflict, coupled with a mismatch between imported solutions and local realities, have left
55.7% of the population facing multidimensional vulnerability. Critical challenges include a
skills gap among youth, inadequate infrastructure in rural regions, an exam-focused
education system that stifles creativity, and cultural pressures steering youth towards
traditional careers or overseas labour. These factors perpetuate cycles of generational
poverty, particularly in post-conflict areas like Batticaloa and Mullaitivu, and plantation
communities like Hatton.

DreamSpace Academy addresses these challenges through its Empowerment Inside School
initiative, targeting 616 students across 9 schools in three regions. The programme
recognises that early intervention is vital to break the "cycle of failure" driving high dropout
rates and disengagement. By integrating challenge-based learning into curricular and
extracurricular activities, it equips students with interdisciplinary skills in STEM, critical
thinking, and problem-solving tailored to local contexts.

The initiative prioritises regions where infrastructure gaps and cultural barriers
disproportionately affect youth. In Mullaitivu, a post-war district, it focuses on rebuilding
innovation capacity disrupted by conflict, while in Hatton, it counters plantation economy
dependencies that trap generations in low-skilled labour. By embedding practical skill
development within school environments, the model aims to cultivate a pipeline of local
changemakers capable of addressing community-specific challenges through grassroots
innovation.

This approach aligns with DreamSpace Academy’s broader mission to combine education,
innovation, and entrepreneurship as interconnected pillars for sustainable development. By
empowering youth to create contextually relevant solutions—rather than relying on externally
imposed fixes—the organisation fosters long-term community ownership and resilience,
laying the groundwork for Sri Lanka to emerge as a self-sustaining hub of social and
technological innovation.

DreamSpace Academy requires a minimum of €5,200 monthly to sustain empowerment
operations, covering salaries for 15 staff members, infrastructure, and educational materials
across regions.

2 INTRODUCTION

2.1 MOTIVATION
Sri Lanka's nearly three-decade civil conflict left profound scars on the nation's
socio-economic landscape and natural ecosystems. While international aid provided crucial
support for post-war recovery, many imported solutions struggled to create lasting impact
due to their fundamental disconnect from local cultural contexts, environmental realities, and
authentic community needs.

A recent UNDP study reveals that 55.7% of Sri Lanka's population faces multidimensional
vulnerability across education, health, and living standards (UNDP 2023). Youth
unemployment, stark regional inequalities, and limited access to innovation resources
remain pressing barriers to sustainable development.

Observing numerous governmental, local, and international civil society organisations
emerge across Sri Lanka to bridge these critical gaps through community-driven
transformation has profoundly inspired us to address these challenges through our own
distinctive approach and innovative methodology.


2.2 PROBLEM
Despite Sri Lanka's human development progress, deep-rooted challenges continue
hindering local innovation, youth empowerment, and inclusive economic growth. Through
extensive fieldwork since 2013 and collaboration with civil society organisations, we've
identified these critical barriers preventing local communities from building meaningful
innovations and impact ventures beyond poverty alleviation.


2.2.1 HUMAN RESOURCES GAP
Many young people and early career professionals lack the essential skills, mentorship, and
practical experience required to engage meaningfully in innovation and entrepreneurship
ecosystems. Whilst raw talent exists abundantly, it remains largely underdeveloped or
misaligned with contemporary opportunities. Furthermore, ambitious youth predominantly
gravitate towards the IT sector, which alone cannot address today's multifaceted challenges.
Modern problems demand interdisciplinary expertise spanning science, engineering, deep
technology, sociology, environmental studies, and numerous other domains to create
comprehensive, sustainable solutions.


2.2.2 INFRASTRUCTURE LIMITATIONS
Access to digital tools, laboratories, makerspaces, and quality learning environments
remains severely constrained, particularly in rural and post-conflict regions. Without
adequate infrastructure, young people cannot engage in hands-on learning, innovation, or
digital creation. Many schools in underserved communities lack fundamental

amenities—functional toilets, clean drinking water, safe access roads, reliable transport,
basic furniture, and electricity.


2.2.3 RIGID EDUCATION SYSTEM
Sri Lanka's education model emphasises rigid subject-based learning with minimal space for
creativity, interdisciplinary approaches, or practical application. Students are conditioned to
pass examinations rather than tackle real-world problems or develop entrepreneurial
thinking. The system systematically fails to cultivate essential soft skills—critical thinking,
media literacy, financial literacy, effective communication, conflict resolution, time and
resource management, expectation handling, and collaborative teamwork—all fundamental
competencies for succeeding in today's interconnected world.


2.2.4 CULTURAL BARRIERS
Intense pressure from families, schools, and society pushes youth towards traditional
careers (doctor and engineer). These expectations discourage exploration of unconventional
fields like science, entrepreneurship, or innovation. Underprivileged youth often face forced
early employment to support families—young men becoming construction workers and
women domestic workers in Middle Eastern countries. Additionally, Sri Lankan diaspora’s
peer pressure drives uneducated youth towards illegal migration to Western countries,
seeking asylum and remaining trapped in mundane jobs without a career advancement.


2.2.5 CYCLE OF FAILURE
These overlapping challenges create cycles of early failure, school dropout, and
disconnection from meaningful opportunities, especially affecting disadvantaged
communities. Plantation workers in central Sri Lanka find themselves trapped in the same
circumstances generation after generation, whilst youngsters from underserved communities
who leave education for overseas labour jobs remain stuck in the same positions throughout
their lives. This creates a pattern where disadvantage passes from parents to children and
keeping vulnerable people from progressing.


2.3 SOLUTION
Drawing from extensive fieldwork experience and collaborative learnings with numerous
social enterprises and community innovation hubs across the Global South and Europe
since 2013, DreamSpace Academy was established in 2018 as a non-profit social enterprise
with a mission to achieve sustainable development goals in Sri Lanka by empowering
underserved communities through education, innovation and entrepreneurship.

We recognised that empowering locals to address their socioeconomic and environmental
challenges requires comprehensive interdisciplinary skills. Therefore, education became
DSA's foundational pillar. We adopted challenge-based learning methodologies where
participants initially acquire only the interdisciplinary skills essential for tackling their specific
challenges, unlike conventional subject-based approaches. We believe that those directly
experiencing challenges are uniquely positioned to develop authentic solutions. This



 DreamSpace Academy | www.dreamspace.academy                                         Concept Report | 8
contrasts sharply with traditional international aid models, where affected communities
typically receive externally developed solutions created without meaningful local participation
or input.

Education alone proved insufficient for addressing local challenges effectively. Therefore,
innovation became DSA's second pillar. We adopted grassroots innovation approaches to
develop sustainable solutions for local challenges, contrasting with expensive imported
black-box technologies requiring substantial financial resources for maintenance, repairs,
and upgrades. When communities create their own solutions, these innovations thrive in
resource-constrained environments while empowering locals to perform repairs when
needed and implement enhancements during upgrades, ensuring long-term viability and
community ownership.

However, complex challenges like environmental issues demand technologically
sophisticated solutions. For a non-profit social enterprise operating in underserved and
war-affected regions, accessing such specialised knowledge and infrastructure presents
significant obstacles. We adopted an open innovation model, leveraging deep tech expertise
and affordable tools from global citizen science organisations to establish diverse
interdisciplinary laboratories locally. This approach enables us to bridge the gap between
advanced technological capabilities and grassroots implementation needs.

Innovation alone cannot sustain knowledge, skills, and local changemakers—sustainable
businesses are essential. Therefore, entrepreneurship became DSA's third pillar. We
adopted impact-venture building approaches to create meaningful social enterprises in
underserved regions for achieving SDGs, unlike privileged capital cities where startups
primarily pursue economic gains for founding shareholders. Many modern Sri Lankan
companies focus on providing software services to foreign markets, while importing scientific
and deeptech solutions for local problems. Consequently, we established incubation centres
providing mentorship, funding, critical infrastructure, and business training for local
changemakers and their social enterprises, ensuring sustainable impact through locally
rooted entrepreneurial ventures.

From 2018 to 2025, DSA pursued these objectives, primarily targeting individuals above 18.
This presented significant challenges in training and retaining participants for several
reasons: graduates from traditional rigid education systems struggled to unlearn and relearn;
many from underserved communities urgently needed immediate employment before
acquiring any professional skills or completing university degrees; social pressure
encouraged overseas unskilled labour jobs offering higher earnings than educated positions;
many participants were first-generation white-collar workers, making professional discipline
challenging; parents and society didn't recognise our activities as equivalent to traditional
institutional degrees. Despite significant efforts in developing skills, innovations, and
ventures, participants frequently returned to the same cycles we aimed to break, as
intervening at this age proved late for long-lasting transformation.

Therefore, we recognised the need to target much younger age groups, enabling earlier
mindset change. We also decided to integrate our activities within existing educational
systems by empowering youngsters directly at their schools alongside curricular

education—an approach accepted by schools, parents, and society as legitimate pathways
to success. Consequently, DSA restructured its activities into two complementary
approaches: empowering youngsters within school environment and empowering them
outside school environment, ensuring comprehensive support while maintaining institutional
credibility and community acceptance.

3 EMPOWERMENT INSIDE SCHOOL
Due to the challenges of training and retaining youth from underserved communities above
18, we decided to refocus our primary empowerment activities on younger students starting
from 12 years old. Our new approach features sustained six-year learning engagement,
starting with Grade 6 students and progressing through Grade 11, guaranteeing consistent
support throughout these critical educational years. This strategic shift allows us to work
within their school environment, improving their curricular performance while introducing
them to our methodologies through structured co-curricular and extracurricular activities.

The Empowerment Inside School initiative specifically targets underserved schools and
disadvantaged students, ensuring that all programmes are provided as a fully funded, free
opportunity. This removes financial barriers and guarantees equal access to transformative
educational experiences for those who need it most.


3.1 CURRICULAR ACTIVITIES
To achieve our goals, we identified Information and Communications Technology (ICT) as
the ideal curriculum subject that provides both essential technology skills and concrete job
opportunities upon successful completion. ICT has recently become a core component of Sri
Lankan school education, integrated into the curriculum from Grade 6 and offered as a main
subject for G.C.E. (O/L) and G.C.E. (A/L). The government has reinforced this priority
through key policies including the National Digital Transformation Policy for Education (2022)
and National Digital Economy Strategy 2030, all aimed at preparing students for the digital
economy.

ICT skills have become a primary requirement for most entry-level white-collar jobs in Sri
Lanka, even for candidates without higher education degrees. By empowering students to
excel in ICT from Grade 6 through O/L and beyond, we can ensure they have better
livelihood opportunities and can break out of poverty cycles—even if they don't continue with
our innovation and entrepreneurial pathways toward achieving sustainable development
goals. This foundational support also satisfies the expectations of parents, schools, and
society.

ICT is fundamentally a skills-based, interdisciplinary subject that requires STEM (science,
technology, engineering, mathematics) knowledge alongside English language proficiency
and soft skills. This makes it the perfect vehicle for our comprehensive empowerment
approach. Additionally, the widespread lack of trained ICT teachers and modern
infrastructure in target schools provides us with the opportunity to secure official permission
from local government to engage directly with schools within their own environments.


3.2 CO-CURRICULAR ACTIVITIES
While strengthening students' ICT curricular performance to ensure exam success, we
introduce transformative challenge-based learning methodologies that shift focus from

hands-on experiences. At the institutional level, we cultivate innovation by establishing
dedicated innovation labs and facilitating student-led projects that foster curiosity and
experimentation. We seamlessly integrate entrepreneurship education into the academic
framework, teaching students to transform ideas into actionable solutions while building
resilience and developing essential entrepreneurial mindsets. Additionally, we provide
comprehensive long-term support for high-potential students, implementing early talent
identification systems and creating structured pathways for continuous learning and
mentorship opportunities.


3.3 EXTRA CURRICULAR ACTIVITIES
For exceptionally motivated students with a supporting family, we provide access to
advanced infrastructure and support beyond their school environments to deepen their
innovation and entrepreneurship capabilities. These selected students connect with
experienced international mentors and innovators through our extensive global network,
fostering collaborative learning and cross-cultural exchange. This comprehensive approach
creates a seamless progression pathway from foundational school-level innovation activities
to sophisticated entrepreneurial development, ensuring talented students can reach their full
potential.

3.6 SELECTED SCHOOLS AND REGIONS
The Empowerment Inside School activities target schools in Batticaloa, Mullaitivu, and
Hatton where DreamSpace Academy currently operates. These selected schools face
severe infrastructure barriers including non-functional computer labs, unreliable electricity,
and poor internet connectivity. The digital divide is particularly acute in estate and rural areas
with inadequate facilities, poor sanitation, and insufficient teachers.

A critical shortage of qualified ICT teachers leaves students with limited access to digital
education. Schools depend on traditional teaching methods with limited active learning
experiences. Additionally, students lack exposure to STEM, robotics, prototyping, and
innovation approaches essential for developing 21st century skills.

These challenges have resulted in poor academic performance, with some schools
experiencing 70-90% O/L examination failure rates and high dropout rates, creating a vicious
cycle where students lose faith in education's value. Therefore, our strategic approach
targets 9 carefully selected schools across 3 locations, with plans to organically scale
operations to numerous additional schools and locations nationwide.


3.6.1 BATTICALOA
The participating schools are located in rural and coastal areas of Batticaloa District in
eastern Sri Lanka. Originally family-run institutions, these schools were later converted to
provincial government schools with severely limited resources. Over 75% of students come
from families where parents have little to no formal education, meaning children receive
minimal academic support at home.

The 2004 tsunami devastated these communities, forcing many families to relocate and
causing school enrolment to plummet. While these schools once had thriving student
populations and strong community support, the disaster created lasting educational
disruption that continues to affect learning opportunities today.




 DreamSpace Academy | www.dreamspace.academy                                     Concept Report | 13
Families face ongoing economic hardship as parents work in daily wage jobs like fishing,
construction, and small-scale labour, earning barely enough for basic survival. This means
families cannot afford private tuition or additional learning resources. Students have virtually
no access to digital tools or modern educational methods, while schools struggle with a
shortage of qualified teachers, particularly in ICT.


3.6.2 MULLAITIVU
The participating schools are located in war-torn and tsunami-affected areas of Mullaitivu
district in the north-eastern Sri Lanka, where these historical traumas have created severe
challenges for educational development. The population consists primarily of fishermen,
farmers, and daily wage Labourers who continue to face regular exposure to natural
disasters, making it extremely difficult to provide consistent, quality education for students.

Rural schools in Mullaitivu suffer from severe teacher shortages, particularly in specialised
subjects like ICT. Despite strong student interest in technology subjects, schools often rely
on teachers from other districts or assign non-specialist teachers to cover ICT classes. This
results in inadequate instruction as teachers lack proper knowledge of the subjects they're
teaching.

The teacher shortage creates a domino effect where students receive poor subject
guidance, especially when choosing their academic paths after Grade 9. Without proper
counselling and instruction, even talented students struggle to make informed decisions
about their future studies and career directions.


3.6.3 HATTON
The participating schools are located in estate communities of Hatton in Nuwara Eliya
District in central Sri Lanka, where families working on plantations face severe
socio-economic hardships that directly undermine their children's education. Living in
overcrowded line houses with inadequate water, sanitation, and electricity, these poor
conditions significantly impact children's health and learning capacity. The economic
pressures force many students to contribute to household duties and income generation,
resulting in irregular school attendance and alarmingly high dropout rates.

A critical challenge is the lack of English language skills, which severely limits students'
academic success and communication abilities. This language gap prevents them from
understanding science and technology subjects and accessing digital learning resources.
The situation is worsened by schools operating with minimal infrastructure—many lack ICT
teachers, computer labs, internet access, and even basic classroom facilities.

Teachers are overextended and unable to provide individual attention or use modern
teaching methods. The absence of proper English instruction is particularly damaging, as it
blocks students from engaging with subject-matter and participating in the global digital
economy.

4 EMPOWERMENT OUTSIDE SCHOOL
Since 2018, DreamSpace Academy has conducted diverse empowerment activities beyond
traditional institutional frameworks. These initiatives include capacity building, innovation
development, impact venture building and incubation, training of trainers (ToT),
peacebuilding, well-being advocacy, rural development, environmental protection, arts and
cultural development, and women empowerment through workshops, bootcamps, projects,
exhibitions, and conferences.

These activities served students, educators, entrepreneurs, eager to learn, innovate, and
create social impact. Conducted at DreamSpace Academy's physical hubs and partner
locations across Sri Lanka, these programs unlocked the potential of numerous local
changemakers who generated a ripple effect of positive change, driving innovations,
enterprises, and success stories that fostered sustainable transformation within their
communities. Furthermore, business ventures launched through these activities continue to
support DreamSpace Academy by providing services, materials, and financial contributions
according to their capacity.

The Empowerment Outside School initiative specifically targets highly motivated participants
who have time outside their school, university, or work commitments and can afford a
minimal fee. Exceptional participants facing economic barriers will receive scholarships or
sponsorships provided by our funding partners.

Therefore, these activities are self-sustaining and generate a minimal income for
DreamSpace Academy, enabling us to continue our mission to achieve sustainable
development goals through education, innovation and entrepreneurship.

We envision to create a thriving ecosystem where changemakers co-create to address
real-world challenges, making Sri Lanka a leading hub for social and technology innovation
in South Asia.
