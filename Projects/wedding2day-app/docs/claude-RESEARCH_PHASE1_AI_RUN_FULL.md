## 1. Executive summary

**The current locked W2D mechanic is not well supported by the available evidence. It is not obviously fatal either — but the burden of proof has not been met, and the specific thing it is betting on is the weakest-supported assumption in the whole concept.**

Four findings drive that:

1. **The mechanic W2D has locked is a copy of a model whose economics are publicly visible — and they are hard.** "Post a listing, reveal a phone number, move to WhatsApp" is IndiaMART's model. IndiaMART is 30 years old, profitable (FY26 revenue ₹1,569 cr, ~40% EBITDA margin), has 8.2m free listings and total SEO dominance — and it is still **losing paying suppliers**: 220,000 paying suppliers at Q4 FY26 after a net loss of 1,200 in the quarter, then 218,000 in Q1 FY27 after a further net decline of ~1,850, with management attributing it to **elevated churn in the entry-level Silver tier, running 4–7% per month**. Entry-tier suppliers churn out of phone-reveal lead products because they cannot see ROI. W2D would be starting at the entry tier, in one state, in one seasonal category, with none of IndiaMART's moats.

2. **The discovery problem W2D is solving may not exist at the size assumed — and the coordination problem it is *not* solving may not exist either.** An exhaustive public-evidence hunt (30+ queries across Reddit, Quora, YouTube, trade press, MSME/academic sources, Tamil-language search) found **no first-person account anywhere of an Indian wedding decorator describing how they choose between manufacturers.** Not one. The circumstantial evidence leans mildly against a discovery gap (directory infrastructure for this category has existed for 25+ years; Justdial already runs separate Chennai pages for "Pandal Decorators," "Tent Dealers" and "Decoration Material Dealers"), but that lean is weak and should not be treated as a finding. **Both the founder's current hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis are currently unevidenced.** This is the single most important sentence in the report.

3. **The niche already has direct incumbents, including one doing almost exactly this.** Event Material Hub (eventmaterial.com) is a live pan-India B2B marketplace for event materials — tents, mandaps, backdrops, domes, props, lighting, fabric, furniture — where buyers submit an enquiry and **sellers then contact the buyer directly to negotiate**, with an explicit **zero-commission** policy. That is W2D's locked mechanic, already shipped, already free, already national. Its actual traction is unknown (no funding or scale data found), which cuts both ways: it may be a ghost town, which is itself evidence about the category.

4. **Even if it works, the addressable revenue in Tamil Nadu alone is small enough to matter.** Working from the best supply-side estimate this research could construct (~2,400–2,800 decor-related businesses in TN, itself a low-confidence Fermi figure), a mature 10% paying penetration at IndiaMART-like entry pricing implies roughly **₹75 lakh–₹1 crore of annual revenue**. Even an aggressive 40% penetration lands near **₹3 crore/year**. That is a viable small business; it is not a venture outcome, and it is thin cover for the cost of building and seeding a two-sided network.

**What this does not say.** It does not say the market is fake. Tamil Nadu plausibly hosts ~230,000–250,000 weddings a year, the trade is genuinely fragmented and informal, and no Tamil-language-native B2B tool for it exists — that gap is real. It says the *mechanic* is the weak part, that it is unvalidated in the specific way that matters, and that the evidence needed to decide cannot be obtained from a desk. **The decision is currently un-makeable on data. Roughly twenty-five honest conversations would make it makeable, and that is cheaper than any of the alternatives.**

## 3. Discovery vs. coordination evidence (Task 2)

### Verdict up front: NO EVIDENCE FOUND, either way.

Thirty-plus queries across Reddit, Quora, YouTube, EventFAQs and trade press, IndiaMART/MouthShut review corpora, MSME/academic sources, and Tamil-language search produced **not one first-person account of an Indian wedding decorator describing how they select among manufacturers for a job.** No post of the form "I called three mandap suppliers and picked the one who could deliver by Thursday." Nothing.

### 3.2 Why "absent" here does not mean "does not exist"

Four of the ABSENT rows are access failures, not confirmed voids, and this distinction should not be lost:
- Quora's two most relevant threads were blocked by robots.txt.
- YouTube comment sections — the likeliest place for unfiltered small-business venting — returned HTTP 429 and could not be read at all.
- The "Wedding Decorators of India" Facebook group exists but is behind a login wall.
- **The entire English-language search may be looking in the wrong language.** Tamil Nadu decorators plausibly conduct this discourse in Tamil, on WhatsApp and Facebook, where it is invisible to web search by construction.

### 3.3 What this means for the decision

- The founder's suspicion that the discovery hypothesis is unsupported is correct — there is no public evidence for it.
- But the coordination hypothesis is equally unsupported. Pivoting from an unevidenced hypothesis to another unevidenced hypothesis is not progress; it is a lateral move that feels like progress.
- The one piece of real survey data found (KPMG, n = event-management firms) points at neither — it points at labour.
- The strongest shape of a signal found (the fabric supplier's framing) describes coordination, but it is marketing copy from a company that profits from that framing being true.

**This question is not answerable from a desk. It is answerable in about two weeks of phone calls.** Sections 7 and 8 exist to make those calls cheap.

## 4. Marketplace failure/success patterns, and how W2D scores (Tasks 3–4)

### 4.1 Documented failure patterns

| # | Pattern | Description |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Disintermediation / leakage | Buyer and seller exchange contact details after the platform-brokered match, then transact directly forever. Leakage is suppressed mainly by capturing payment and bundling services the parties can't replicate offline. High-ticket B2B is the most leakage-prone category. (Hagiu & Wright, *Marketplace Leakage*, Management Science 2023) |
| F2 | Chicken-and-egg liquidity | Two-sided cold start. Worse in B2B than consumer: supplier onboarding is a months-long sales motion with no guaranteed demand on the other side. (NFX Marketplace Scorecard; Version One Marketplace Handbook) |
| F3 | Thin unit economics of pure matchmaking | Pure lead-gen/listing monetises at low single-digit effective take; after support and CAC, contribution margin is negative for years. ShopX: 8–12% gross margin vs $50–80 CAC per store, 60% of onboarded stores inactive within a month. |
| F4 | Low transaction frequency | Gurley's Factor 8: frequency drives word-of-mouth, brand recall and CAC amortisation. Low-frequency and seasonal categories struggle to build habit. (Bill Gurley, *All Markets Are Not Created Equal*) |
| F5 | Low frequency destroys retention loops | Low-frequency marketplaces cannot rely on habit and must pick one of four alternatives: SEO dominance, 10x-better product, an urgency/"insurance" hook, or a high-frequency engagement layer between transactions (the Houzz model). (Casey Winters) |
| F6 | Marketplace-of-one / trusted-incumbent lock-in | In relationship-driven cash trades, once a buyer has one reliable supplier they sole-source and stop shopping. Indian agritech hit this: the middleman being disintermediated was also providing credit and trust, which an app cannot replicate. (Ninjacart pivot coverage) |
| F7 | Multi-homing | Participants use several platforms at once, diluting network effects and pricing power. (Zhu & Iansiti, HBR) |
| F8 | GMV/vanity-metric chasing | Optimising listings and GMV rather than buyer/seller happiness produces something that looks big and isn't defensible. (Sarah Tavel, *Hierarchy of Marketplaces*) |
| F9 | Premature expansion before unit economics | RenoRun: $200m+ raised, $77m revenue, insolvent 2023 after 6-city, 2-country expansion. |

### 4.2 Documented success patterns

| # | Pattern | Description |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Full-stack / managed | Stop matchmaking; own fulfilment, QC and pricing. Become the industry rather than sell software to it. (a16z, *The Full-Stack Startup*) |
| S2 | Vertical concentration | Narrow focus builds density and trust faster than breadth. (Sarah Tavel) |
| S3 | SaaS-enabled: "come for the tool, stay for the network" | Ship a standalone-useful tool (quoting, inventory, scheduling) that a supplier would want with zero network effect; layer the network on later. Standard solution to cold start. (Point Nine) |
| S4 | Embedded credit as moat | SME buyers in fragmented trade are credit-constrained; whoever supplies working capital owns the relationship. OfBusiness/Oxyzo reportedly runs ~0.5% NPAs vs 4–5% industry, on transaction-data underwriting. |
| S5 | Payment capture as the anti-leakage mechanism | The single most direct lever against F1, in both academic and practitioner literature. |
| S6 | Trust / verification / status loops | Verified badges, response rates, ratings that sort good supply to the top and make the ranking itself valuable. (Sarah Tavel) |
| S7 | Layer-cake take rate | Take rate tracks how much of the workflow you own. Pure matching ≈ 1–2%; ACV Auctions went from ~2% to 4%+ by adding financing, logistics, data. |

### 4.3 Indian case evidence

| Company | Model | Outcome | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IndiaMART** | Lead-gen directory, phone reveal, seller subscription | FY26 revenue ₹1,569 cr, ~40% EBITDA. But paying suppliers 220,000 (Q4 FY26, net −1,200), falling to 218,000 (Q1 FY27, net −1,850); Silver-tier churn 4–7% per month; unique business enquiries −11% YoY to 26m. Platinum/Gold = 75%+ of revenue and retain well. | The phone-reveal model is profitable at scale for the incumbent and brutal at the entry tier. Small suppliers churn because they can't see ROI. |
| **Udaan** | Marketplace + logistics + NBFC | Valuation $3.1bn → $1.75bn (−43.5%); GMV ₹9,900 cr → ₹5,629 cr; three layoff rounds; pivoted toward credit/services | Trading GMV doesn't pay; credit does |
| **ShopX** | Horizontal B2B marketplace | Insolvent 2022 | F3 in its purest form |
| **Solv (Standard Chartered)** | B2B marketplace + trade finance | Acquired by Jumbotail (2025) | Even bank-backed, credit-armed marketplaces struggled to stand alone |
| **Zetwerk / Infra.Market / OfBusiness / Moglix** | Full-stack: own manufacturing, distribution and/or credit | All reached multi-billion scale; Zetwerk DRHP filed | S1 + S4 is the pattern that actually worked in Indian fragmented trade |
| **Ninjacart** | Disintermediate agri middlemen | Ongoing pivot; sector funding collapsed post-2021 | F6 — the middleman was selling trust and credit, not just matching |
| **Meragi** | Wedding decor/services, $9.1m Accel-led | Expanding into managed "experience centres" | The one funded player in W2D's actual category is moving away from aggregation, toward full-stack |
| **ShaadiSaga / Weddingz.in / Bookeventz** | Wedding aggregators | ₹11 cr acquisition / absorbed by OYO then deprioritised / flat | The realistic ceiling for subscale wedding lead-gen |

### 4.4 Scoring the locked W2D mechanic

**The mechanic under test:** post listing or requirement → counterparty taps → phone number revealed → conversation moves to WhatsApp. No chat, no payments, no booking, no workflow in-app.

| Pattern | W2D exposure | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 Disintermediation | Maximal — by design | FAILS | The mechanic's stated purpose is to hand over a phone number and exit. There is no payment to capture (S5 unavailable), no workflow to retain, no reason to return. **The product is a leak with a login screen.** |
| F2 Chicken-and-egg | High, with no unlock | FAILS | Nothing in the mechanic is useful to a single user alone. S3 ("come for the tool") is the standard escape and W2D has explicitly excluded every candidate tool. |
| F3 Thin matchmaking economics | High | FAILS | Pure lead-gen is the thinnest B2B model there is. IndiaMART needs 220,000 paying suppliers across every industry in India to make ₹1,569 cr. TN wedding decor supports a few hundred realistic payers (§5.4). |
| F4 / F5 Low frequency | Severe, worse than baseline | FAILS | Weddings are low-frequency and muhurtham-clustered into a handful of auspicious dates. With no engagement layer between transactions, there is no reason to open the app for months at a stretch. |
| F6 Marketplace-of-one | High | FAILS | Cash-heavy, relationship-driven, trust-and-credit-mediated trade — the textbook F6 environment. Once matched, the relationship exits the platform permanently. |
| F7 Multi-homing | High | WEAK | The same manufacturer will keep IndiaMART, Justdial, Sulekha, Event Material Hub and WhatsApp. W2D gives them no reason to consolidate. |
| F8 Vanity metrics | Structural risk | WEAK | The only metrics this mechanic produces are listings and reveals. Neither correlates with anyone making money. |
| F9 Premature expansion | Not yet triggered | OK | TN-only is correctly narrow. |
| S1 Full-stack | Absent | NOT PRESENT | Explicitly excluded |
| S2 Vertical concentration | Present | PRESENT | One vertical, one state, founder has 10+ years of domain credibility. This is the concept's genuine strength. |
| S3 Come for the tool | Absent | NOT PRESENT | No tool. Nothing standalone-useful. |
| S4 Embedded credit | Absent | NOT PRESENT | Excluded — though this is where the Indian success cases actually won |
| S5 Payment capture | Absent | NOT PRESENT | Excluded. This is the specific remedy for the specific failure the mechanic is most exposed to. |
| S6 Trust/verification | Latent and unused | PARTIAL | A listing implies some profile, but the locked spec has no verification, no ratings, no response rates. Cheapest available upgrade, not in the spec. |
| S7 Layer-cake take rate | Bottom rung | NOT PRESENT | Owns matching only — the 1–2% rung |

**Tally: 8 failure patterns triggered, 1 avoided; 1 success pattern present, 1 partial, 5 absent.**

### 4.5 Plain statement

**The locked mechanic is closer to a failure pattern than a success pattern, and it is not close.**

The precise problem is not that phone-reveal is a stupid idea — it is a profitable idea for IndiaMART. The problem is that phone-reveal is the endgame of a business that has already won distribution, not the opening move of one that hasn't. IndiaMART can run a leaky matching product because it owns search intent for all of Indian B2B and monetises 220,000 suppliers across hundreds of categories; the leak is affordable when the top of the funnel is infinite and free. W2D would have a leak of identical size and a funnel roughly the width of one state's wedding-decoration trade — and it is choosing to compete on the incumbent's own mechanic while giving up the two levers (payment capture, embedded workflow) that the literature identifies as the only reliable defences.

Two contextual facts sharpen this. First, IndiaMART's own entry tier — the tier W2D would be selling into — is churning at 4–7% per month. Second, Event Material Hub already ships W2D's mechanic nationally at zero commission. W2D would be entering as a paid, single-state version of a free, national product.

**One honest counter-argument, stated fairly.** Every objection above is about the mechanic. None of it disproves the market. A locally-trusted, Tamil-language, founder-credible network in a trade where nothing native exists is a real asset, and it is possible that in a market this relationship-dense the "leak" is not fatal because the platform's value was never the transaction — it was being the place where the trade's roster lives, the way a trade association is. That argument is coherent. It is also completely untested, and the only way to test it is to talk to the people concerned.

### 5.2 Tamil Nadu wedding volume — derived, not sourced

No official TN wedding count exists in any accessible source. SRS 2021 does not publish a crude marriage rate; no CRS state-level marriage report was found; the TN Registration Department publishes no annual statistics.

| Step | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| TN population, 2026 | 77.58m | Technical Group on Population Projections 2011–2036, MoHFW |
| Assumed marriage events per 1,000 population | ~3 | Rule of thumb, unverified. No authoritative Indian CMR was located. |
| ⇒ TN weddings/year | ~233,000 | 77.58m × 3/1000 |
| Cross-check via CAIT's 46 lakh weddings × TN population share | ~246,000 | Circular — inherits CAIT's grade-C base |

**Use ~230,000–250,000 TN weddings/year as an order of magnitude only. ±30–40% error bars are not unreasonable.**

**Seasonality is a first-order product constraint, not a footnote.** Tamil weddings cluster on muhurtham dates from the Panchangam; only a limited set of days each year qualify, whole months (e.g. Aadi) are avoided. Consequences: demand arrives in bursts; capacity, not price, is likely the binding constraint on peak dates; and a low-frequency app becomes an extremely low-frequency app outside those windows. This is the strongest structural argument in the whole report for a capacity/availability product and against a static listings board.

**Read these counts correctly.** Listings are not businesses; directories mix fabricators, traders and decorators indiscriminately; dead accounts are not pruned. Most importantly: 39 wedding-decoration listings for the whole of Tamil Nadu on ExportersIndia, and ~24 in Chennai on IndiaMART, are absurdly low for a state hosting ~230,000 weddings a year. That is not evidence that only ~100 decor businesses exist — it is evidence that the trade is almost entirely absent from national B2B directories. It runs on WhatsApp, word of mouth and physical markets.

This cuts both ways: **for W2D**, incumbent directories have not actually penetrated this trade, so "IndiaMART already does this" is weaker than it looks on paper. **Against W2D**, twenty-five years of well-funded directories with free listings and national SEO failed to pull this trade online — a pre-seed app with the same mechanic should explain what it knows that they didn't.

| Scenario | Paying accounts | ARPU | TN annual revenue |

| 10% penetration, entry-tier price | ~260 | ₹28,000 | ~₹73 lakh |

**Implication, stated plainly:** on a pure lead-gen model, Tamil Nadu is a ₹1–3 crore/year business at maturity, if everything goes right. That can be an excellent owner-operated company. It cannot carry a venture-scale build, and it will not survive a price war with a free national incumbent. Anything larger requires either geographic expansion (F9 risk) or moving up the take-rate layer cake (S7).

## 6. Alternative product directions worth testing (Task 6)

These are options, not recommendations. Each is presented with the condition under which it makes sense, so the founder can kill the ones whose precondition the interviews disprove. D1–D3 target coordination; D4–D5 sidestep the discovery-vs-coordination question entirely by attacking constraints the research surfaced independently.

### D1 — Structured broadcast RFQ ("one requirement, many quotes, side by side")

A decorator posts a structured requirement — item, spec, quantity, delivery date, pin code, budget band — broadcast to matching manufacturers, who reply inside the app with a price, available-by date, photo. Decorator sees a comparison grid, not a list of phone numbers.

**What changes vs. the locked mechanic:** the unit of value moves from a contact to a comparison. A phone number is worth having once; a comparison is worth having every job.

**Defensibility:** partial but real. The decorator can and will WhatsApp the winner — but the next job needs a fresh comparison. Quote history accrues and is not portable to WhatsApp. **Weakness:** if a decorator has 2–3 trusted suppliers and only ever wants those, this collapses into a group chat and loses.

**Precondition to test:** decorators genuinely price-shop more than 2–3 suppliers per job.

### D2 — Capacity and availability calendar keyed to muhurtham dates

Manufacturers/rental houses publish what is free on which dates against the Tamil muhurtham calendar. Decorators search by date first, item second.

**Why:** §5.2 established TN weddings pile onto a small set of auspicious dates. On those dates the binding constraint is likely availability, not price or discovery — a question WhatsApp answers badly (ringing seven people serially, hoping).

**Defensibility:** strong, structurally — the data has a shelf life. A phone number is permanently valuable once revealed; "free on 14 November" is worthless in December. The supplier must keep updating it, so they keep coming back; leakage doesn't help because the thing you'd leak decays.

**Weakness:** requires suppliers to maintain a calendar — real work, the classic reason such products die. Mitigation: one-tap ("block these dates"), and decorators searching by date only see suppliers with fresh calendars.

**Precondition to test:** peak-date capacity crunch is a real, named, painful experience decorators can describe unprompted.

### D3 — Group / pooled requirement posting

Multiple decorators with the same need in the same window aggregate into one order to reach a manufacturer's MOQ or unlock bulk price.

**Why:** the only articulation of a coordination-shaped tradeoff found in public sources (Vmore fabrics) is precisely bulk-price-vs-local-convenience.

**Defensibility:** strong in principle — W2D would hold information no participant can obtain alone, the textbook definition of a defensible network position.

**Weakness:** hardest to operate. Requires coordinating delivery, splitting payment, assigning liability on default. Requires decorators to cooperate with direct competitors. Highest-ceiling, highest-execution-risk option.

**Precondition to test:** MOQs or bulk-price breaks actually bite, and decorators are already informally sharing orders.

### D4 — Verified production-capacity profiles + trust layer

Not a directory of names — a verified record: factory photos, machinery, monthly output capacity, past job references, GST verification, on-time-delivery track record, response time. The founder's 10+ years in the trade is the verification asset.

**Why:** both IndiaMART complaints found were about noise and fake leads, not absence of listings — a signal problem. S6 (trust/verification loops) is the cheapest unused success lever, and the one W2D is uniquely positioned to pull.

**Defensibility:** moderate. Compounds — a two-year delivery record can't be copied by a new entrant — but is a one-time value delivery per supplier. **Works best as a layer under D1 or D2, not standalone.**

**Precondition to test:** decorators have actually been burned by an unreliable supplier and can tell the story.

### D5 — Come for the tool, stay for the network: a job/quotation tool for decorators

A single-player tool a decorator would use even if no other user existed: build a quotation, itemise decor, track the job's material list, record what was rented from whom, track advances and balances. Supplier network added later as autofill.

**Why:** this is S3, the standard solution to F2 (chicken-and-egg), otherwise unsolvable for W2D. Also acquires the frequency the wedding vertical structurally lacks — a decorator opens a quoting tool weekly even in a slow season. Produces the underwriting data for S4 (credit) as a byproduct.

**Defensibility:** highest of the five — inverts the leak problem, since there's nothing to disintermediate. The decorator's own job records live in the app and get more valuable the longer they use it.

**Weakness:** not a marketplace, not what the founder set out to build. Network is year two, not month three. Competes with pen-and-paper/WhatsApp notes — free, fully understood, the hardest incumbent there is.

**Precondition to test:** decorators quote in writing at all, and find quoting/tracking annoying.

### Comparison

| | Attacks | Leak resistance | Cold-start difficulty | Ceiling | Founder's edge applies |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Locked mechanic | Discovery | None | High | Low (₹1–3 cr TN) | Partly |
| D1 Broadcast RFQ | Comparison | Medium | Medium-high | Medium | Yes |
| D2 Capacity calendar | Peak-date availability | High (data decays) | Medium | Medium-high | Yes |
| D3 Pooled orders | Bulk price / MOQ | High (unreplicable info) | Highest | High | Yes |
| D4 Verified profiles | Trust / noise | Medium (one-time per supplier) | Low | Low alone; multiplier on D1/D2 | Strongest |
| D5 Quoting tool → network | Daily admin friction | Highest (own data) | Lowest | High, but slow | Yes |

**Two observations, no recommendation.** D4 is cheap, fast, and additive to every other option — plausible regardless of which direction wins. D2 is the only option whose core premise is supported by something this research established independently (muhurtham clustering) rather than an untested behavioural hypothesis.

## 7. Interview guides (Tasks 7–8)

### 7.0 How to run these so the answers are worth having

**The single rule: never mention the app.** Introduce yourself as someone with ten years in decoration manufacturing studying how the trade works. That is true.

1. **Anchor everything to one specific, real, recent job.** Not "usually" — the last actual wedding.
2. **Ask about the past, never the future.** No question in either guide asks what someone would do.
3. **Ask for behaviour, then evidence.** Screenshots beat adjectives.
4. **Money is the only real signal.** What they already pay for tells you more than any stated preference.
5. **Sit in the silence.** The second thing they say is usually the true thing.
6. **Run it in Tamil, and record it.** The public discourse in this trade is invisible to English search — assume the same of the interview.

**Sample:** 10–15 decorators and 10–15 manufacturers across Chennai, Coimbatore and Madurai, deliberately including people the founder does not already know.

**Kill criteria, written down before starting:** if fewer than 3 of 15 decorators, unprompted, describe either a sourcing search or a supplier-comparison episode as a real problem, the discovery and coordination theses are both dead and W2D should not be built on either.

### 7.1 Guide A — Decorators / service providers (13 questions)

**Framing:** "I've been in decoration manufacturing about ten years and I'm trying to understand how decorators on your side actually run a job. I'm not selling anything. Can you walk me through your last wedding?"

**Part 1 — The real job**
1. Tell me about the last wedding you decorated. What was it, where, roughly what scale, what did you charge?
2. Walk me through it from the enquiry to striking the set, in order.
3. What did you need to bring in from outside for that job — mandap, backdrop, lighting, chairs, fabric, props? Owned, rented, or made/bought new?
4. For each outside item: who did you get it from, and how long have you used them?
5. Take the item that gave you the most trouble on that job. Exactly what happened — who you called, in what order, how long it took to settle.
6. Did you check price with anyone else, or go straight to your usual person?
7. Has there been a time in the last year your usual supplier couldn't do it? What happened?
8. Think about your busiest muhurtham day this year. How did you handle materials? What almost went wrong?

**Part 2 — Existing tools**
9. When you needed a supplier you didn't already know, what did you actually do?
10. What do you pay money for today to run this business — listings, ads, staff, apps, subscriptions? Roughly how much a year?

**Part 3 — Time and money**
11. In a busy week, where does your time go that you wish it didn't?
12. What most often costs you money on a job — a mistake, a delay, a price you couldn't get?
13. How do you pay your suppliers — advance, on delivery, after the wedding, credit? Has anyone given you material on credit?

**Optional closer, once, at the end:** "If someone could fix one thing about the supply side of your business tomorrow, what would you want it to be?" Treat as a wish, not evidence.

**Deliberately excluded questions:** "Would you use an app that…?", "Is it hard to find suppliers?", "How much would you pay for…?", "Do you have trouble comparing prices?", "Would you like to see all suppliers in one place?" — all leading or hypothetical.

### 7.2 Guide B — Manufacturers / suppliers (13 questions)

**Framing:** "I've been on the manufacturing side about ten years myself and I'm trying to understand how business is coming to people now compared to before. Can I ask about your last few months?"

**Part 1 — Where orders come from**
1. What do you make/supply, how long, how many people work with you?
2. Tell me about your last three orders — who from, how did each customer first come to you?
3. Of last season's business, roughly what share came from people you already knew vs. new people? (Push for a number — this decides whether discovery is real on the supply side.)
4. Where did the new ones come from?
5. When you have free capacity, what do you do about it? Who do you call?
6. Do you turn work away? When did you last say no, and why?

**Part 2 — Digital channels**
7. Are you listed anywhere online — IndiaMART, Justdial, TradeIndia, Facebook, Instagram, own site? For each: do you pay? How much? When did you last get an actual order from it?
8. If they pay: how many calls a month, how many become orders? Renewing this year?
9. If they don't: have you tried one? What happened?
10. When a decorator asks for a quote, what do you send and how? How long does it take?

**Part 3 — Money and risk**
11. How do decorators pay you — advance, delivery, after the wedding? Do you give credit? To whom, and how did it go?
12. What's the most annoying part of dealing with decorators?
13. What decides whether next year is a good year — more customers, better prices, more capacity, faster payment, something else?

**Optional closer:** "If a decorator you'd never met called tomorrow with a big order, what would you want to know about them first?" — surfaces trust/payment-risk requirements for D4.

### 7.3 How to read the results

| If you hear this | It means |
|---|---|
| Manufacturers say 80%+ of business is repeat/referral and they don't want more customers | Discovery is dead. Kill the locked mechanic. |
| Decorators name 2–3 suppliers and never shop around | Coordination is dead too. D1/D3 lose their basis. Look at D2/D4/D5 or stop. |
| Decorators describe peak-muhurtham scrambling unprompted | D2 has a real basis. |
| "They pay me after the wedding" appears repeatedly | The real business is credit (S4), not matching. |
| Nobody can describe a single sourcing episode as painful | The premise is wrong at the root — a good outcome, costs 2 weeks not 2 years. |

## 9. Recommended next step — for the founder to decide

Development is currently paused. Every option below is a way of un-pausing. The recommendation is about sequence, not what to build.

### The recommendation

**Do not resume building, do not pivot, and do not kill W2D yet. Spend the next two to three weeks on 25 conversations, then decide with data.**

The founder's suspicion that the direction is wrong is well-founded on the mechanic and unfounded on the market. Section 4 shows the locked mechanic triggers 8 of 9 documented failure patterns and carries 1 of 7 success patterns — trust that finding. But section 3 shows there is no public evidence for the replacement hypothesis either — pivoting now to a coordination product would swap one untested belief for another while feeling like progress. That is the most expensive mistake available right now.

Twenty-five interviews cost two to three weeks and no capital. The founder's 10+ years in the trade means he can get these meetings in days, in Tamil, in person. The asymmetry is extreme: the downside of interviewing first is a three-week delay; the downside of building first is a year.

### The concrete plan

| When | Do | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| This week | Call tndecors.org / the tent dealers' association. Post a real requirement on Event Material Hub as a buyer and count what comes back and how fast. Pull the EEMAGINE 2026 Chennai attendee list (convention was 11 days ago — intros still warm). Write down kill criteria from §7.0 first. | Interview list of 25+ names; one live competitor data point |
| Weeks 1–2 | 12–15 decorator interviews (Guide A) across Chennai, Coimbatore, Madurai — include people you don't know | Transcripts, tally of Guide B Q3's repeat-vs-new number |
| Weeks 2–3 | 10–12 manufacturer interviews (Guide B), weighted to Coimbatore FRP and Madurai mandap clusters | Same |
| End of week 3 | Score against §7.3 and the pre-written kill criteria | Continue / pivot to a named direction / kill |

### What the three outcomes look like

| If interviews show | Then |
|---|---|
| Manufacturers want new customers and decorators actively search for suppliers | Discovery thesis survives — but don't ship the locked mechanic as specified. Add D4 (verification) and something that survives the first connection. |
| Decorators shop around, compare, and get stuck comparing | Build D1 (broadcast RFQ), with D4 underneath. |
| Pain is peak-date capacity or late payment | Follow to D2 or a credit product (S4) — a different company than the one currently paused, better learned in week 3 than year 2. |
| Nobody can name a sourcing problem at all | Kill it, and count that as a win. Three weeks spent vs. a year. The founder's domain expertise gets pointed somewhere with evidence behind it. |

### Two things worth doing regardless

- **The Tamil-language angle is a real, verified gap independent of the mechanic.** Every incumbent operates in English. Whatever gets built, build it in Tamil first — a moat national players won't copy for a single state.
- **Give up the "no chat, no payments, no booking" constraint, or stop treating it as locked.** Section 4 is unambiguous that those three exclusions are what convert a plausible product into a structurally leaky one. Something must survive the first phone call, or W2D is paying to introduce people and then getting out of the way — a service, not a business.
