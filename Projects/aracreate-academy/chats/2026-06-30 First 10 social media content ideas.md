# First 10 social media content ideas

## Summary
**Conversation overview**

The person is developing social media content for araCreate Academy, a STEM and robotics education programme that operates inside government and government-aided schools in India, running weekly structured classes for students in grades 5 to 9. Their stated target audiences are school principals (as the primary decision-maker and gatekeeper) and parents. The conversation progressed through several iterations: an initial request for 10 posts without clear direction, a pivot to a five-pillar launch sequence (Who We Are, The Problem, Our Approach, What We Deliver, The Student Journey), and then a deeper rewrite request emphasising psychological marketing principles where every word is considered against the target audience's mindset and behaviour.

The person shared a reference template for tone and structure and explicitly instructed Claude not to copy a single word from it — only to use it as a rhythm and format reference. Claude identified and flagged one panel in the reference example (a NASA-linked credibility claim unconnected to araCreate) and removed it entirely rather than adapt it. The final approved direction was to write copy grounded in plain, warm South Indian English with no AI filler language (specifically: elevate, empower, unlock, future-ready, game-changer), favouring proof over hype and specific over abstract. The person approved adding two additional conversion-focused panels — Track Record and Let's Start With a Demo — bringing the total to seven posts.

The final deliverable structured each post around the specific reader's mindset, the persuasion lever being deployed, final word-for-word caption copy, hashtags, media and photo requirements for the design team, and visual direction notes. Claude also produced a recommended posting sequence with strategic reasoning (build belief → peak desire → kill doubt → offer a safe first step) and flagged two posts (04 and 05) as dependent on real student photography before launch. All content was packaged into a Word document formatted as a design team brief with one table per post. The person's consistent correction pattern across the conversation was to reject outputs that lacked strategic depth or proper structure, pushing Claude toward more deliberate, audience-psychology-driven work each time.

## 01 | WHO WE ARE

**Who's reading:** A parent scrolling, or a principal who clicked your profile after hearing your name. Their unspoken question: *"Is this real, and is this for a school like mine?"*
**Lever:** Belonging + legitimacy. Make the reader feel a school that does this is a *serious* school — without saying "serious."
**Word choices:** Open on what the parent *wants*, not on "the changing world" (abstract, ignored). "With their own hands" is doing real work — it separates you from slideshow vendors in four words.

> Every parent wants their child to do well in a world that keeps changing.
> But no child learns to build things by only reading about them.
> araCreate brings Robotics, AI and STEM into your school — real classes, every week, taught by our own trainers.
> Students don't just hear about technology. They sit down and build it with their own hands.
> Children remember what they build, not what they memorise.

## 02 | THE PROBLEM

**Who's reading:** Principal. They may have a "lab" gathering dust. Their guard is highest here — they don't want to be told their school failed.
**Lever:** Name an external villain (*equipment without structure*), never blame the school. A principal who feels accused stops reading; one who feels understood nods.
**Word choices:** "Already spent money" respects that they tried. "Sitting in a cupboard" is vivid and points at the sector, not at them. The closer quietly positions araCreate as the missing half.

> Many schools have already spent money on computers, kits and smart classrooms.
> But machines kept in a cupboard don't teach a single child anything.
> What's missing is not equipment. It's a trainer, a proper plan, and a class that runs every week.
> That is the exact gap araCreate was built to fill.
> Tools don't change a classroom. People and a plan do.

## 03 | OUR APPROACH

**Who's reading:** Principal burned by one-day workshops + parent who wants to know their child actually progresses.
**Lever:** Structure signals safety. A named, staged system (Learner → Innovator → Changemaker) feels proven, not improvised. The closer attacks the competitor's weak spot directly.
**Word choices:** The swimming line makes "you can't rush this" undeniable in one image. The closer ("a photo" vs "a skill") names the vanity-photo trap most STEM vendors sell — principals have seen exactly that.

> You can't learn robotics in one workshop, the same way you can't learn to swim in one lesson.
> A child needs weeks of practice before the ideas actually stay.
> So we built a year-long path: first a Learner, then an Innovator, then a Changemaker.
> They start with the basics, move on to building real projects, and finish by solving a real problem.
> One workshop gives a photo. One year gives a skill.

## 04 | WHAT WE DELIVER

**Who's reading:** Principal wanting a deliverable they can show management and parents + parent wanting to know what their child *does*.
**Lever:** Concreteness as proof. The tangible end-artifact (a working project the child can explain) is the thing they can point to. Specifics here read as confidence.
**Word choices:** "Comes to your school" carries the weight that *you* don't have to arrange anything. "Fix it themselves when it doesn't work" shows real learning, including struggle — honest, not glossy. The closer hands the reader a test they'll now apply in your favour.

> Every week our trainer comes to your school and runs a hands-on class.
> Students work with electronics, sensors, robots, AI and 3D printing — with their hands, not from a textbook.
> They wire a circuit, write the code, and fix it themselves when it doesn't work.
> By the end of the year, each child has built a working project and can explain how it actually runs.
> A child who can explain what they built has truly learnt it.

## 05 | THE STUDENT JOURNEY

**Who's reading:** Parent, mostly — this is the emotional post. Also the principal, who sees the transformation they can show off.
**Lever:** Before-and-after on one child. A single vivid child beats aggregate numbers for emotion. The arc from "doesn't know what a sensor is" to "explains why it works" is satisfying and believable.
**Word choices:** Start humble and relatable ("don't even know what a sensor is") so every parent thinks *that's my child*. End on quiet pride, not hype. The closer is plain on purpose — overwriting it would kill the feeling.

## 06 | TRACK RECORD

**Who's reading:** The skeptical principal, guard fully up, asking *"Is this actually working anywhere, or am I the experiment?"*
**Lever:** Social proof + third-party validation. The principal doesn't trust *your* claim — they trust the *government's*. SIDP is your strongest card because it's external and official, so it leads. Numbers are stated flat, no adjectives — confident facts don't need decoration.
**Word choices:** "Not a pilot" directly kills the fear of being the test case. "Right now" beats "we have achieved" — present tense feels alive and ongoing. The closer reframes the numbers as the principal's own risk-check, answered.

> 4 schools. 700-plus students. Every week, inside government and aided schools.
> 7 of our students' projects were selected at state level through SIDP — the government's own innovation programme.
> That selection wasn't judged by us. It was judged by people outside araCreate.
> This is not a pilot and you are not the experiment. It is already running, right now.
> The safest school to be is the one that isn't the first.

## 07 | LET'S START WITH A DEMO

**Who's reading:** A principal now interested but not yet committed — the highest-drop-off moment in the whole funnel.
**Lever:** Risk reversal. Every reason to say "not now" is fear of wasting money or looking foolish to management. So you remove the cost, remove the commitment, and hand them the judgement. A free demo isn't a freebie — it's you absorbing 100% of the risk so they absorb none.
**Word choices:** "You don't have to take my word" disarms the natural distrust of a salesperson. The three-beat closer — *you watch, your students build, you decide* — puts the principal in control, which is exactly what a decision-maker needs to feel before saying yes. "Before you decide anything" lowers the stakes of the ask to almost nothing.

> You don't have to take my word for any of this.
> Let us run one free demo class in your school, before you decide anything.
> Your trainers watch from the back. Your students sit down and build something real in that one hour.
> Then you judge it yourself — not from a brochure, but from what you saw with your own eyes.
> You watch, your students build, you decide.

**Posting order — and the reasoning.** This isn't the order you wrote them; it's the order a principal's mind actually moves through a decision:

1. **WHO WE ARE** (01) — establish you're real before anything else.
2. **THE PROBLEM** (02) — make them feel the gap *before* you sell the fix. A problem they feel is a problem they want solved.
3. **OUR APPROACH** (03) — now that they feel the gap, show the structured fix.
4. **WHAT WE DELIVER** (04) — make the fix concrete and tangible.
5. **THE STUDENT JOURNEY** (05) — emotional peak; let them *want* it for a child.
6. **TRACK RECORD** (06) — right after desire peaks, remove doubt with hard proof. Desire then proof is the conversion engine.
7. **LET'S START WITH A DEMO** (07) — strike while belief is highest, with a zero-risk way in.
